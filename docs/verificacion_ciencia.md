# Moon and Mars hardware: verified facts (fact-check, 15 Nov 2026)

Tags: [read] = I read it on the page at that URL. [snippet] = seen only in an official-site search result, page not opened. Use [snippet] items with care or re-check.
Press kits (Pathfinder 1997, Phoenix 2008) are pre-landing documents: used here for hardware and positions only, never for results.
Quote marks: wording taken from PDFs I extracted as text (NASA Facts 2001, Pathfinder fact sheet, the press kits, JPL TR 32-1035) is verbatim. Other quoted wording passed through a page-summary tool, so check it on the page before printing it as a quotation.

## 1. Surveyor 1 (Moon, landed 2 Jun 1966)

### DISCOVERY: the legs, footpads and strain gauges showed the Moon would hold up a lander
- **Part:** three landing legs, each with a shock absorber, crushable honeycomb aluminium blocks and a footpad with a crushable bottom; strain gauges spread through the structure. [read] https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1966-045A
  - NASA Science puts the shock absorbers and strain gauges on the landing pads, "to record landing characteristics" important for Apollo. [read] https://science.nasa.gov/mission/surveyor-1/
  - The JPL landing-dynamics report puts the strain gauges on the leg shock absorbers (its Fig. 10, "Surveyor I shock absorber strain-gage"). [read] JPL TR 32-1035, Sperling & Garba, 15 Aug 1967, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19670024848/downloads/19670024848.pdf (citation page https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19670024848)
- **What it did:** some scientists feared the lander would sink into deep dust. Pictures of the footpads showed it had not: it rested on a firm, supportive surface. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/fifty-years-of-moon-dust-surveyor-1-was-a-pathfinder-for-apollo/
- **Numbers (two NASA sources disagree; both are only a few centimetres):**
  - JPL TR 32-1035: best estimates are Pad 2 about 2.0 in (about 5 cm) and Pad 3 about 1.8 in (about 4.6 cm); Pad 1 was outside the camera's view. [read] (URL above)
  - NASA Facts "Did U.S. Astronauts Really Land on the Moon?" (reissued 14 Feb 2001): the footpad "had sunk in less than one inch". [read] https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/PDFs/lunar_landing.pdf
  - Same JPL report, matching the strain-gauge telemetry: soil bearing strength of 0–2 psi at the surface and 6–8 psi at a depth of 2 in. So the surface is soft on top and firmer just below. [read]
- **What humanity learned:** Apollo planners needed to know whether the surface could hold the 33,500-pound Apollo lunar module. Surveyor 1's footpads helped answer that. [read] JPL 2016 URL above.

### QUESTION
- **Setup:** before 1966 some scientists worried that the Moon was covered in deep, fluffy dust. After landing, Surveyor 1 turned its TV camera on its own footpad. How deep had the footpad sunk?
- **A)** So deep it disappeared: the Moon is covered in metres of dust.
- **B)** Only a few centimetres: the ground was firm enough to hold a spacecraft. **(correct)**
- **C)** Not at all: it sat on bare rock and left no mark.
- **Explanation:** the pads pressed only a few centimetres into the soil: about 5 cm by JPL's 1967 analysis, or "less than one inch" by a 2001 NASA fact sheet. Either figure gives B. The soil was soft on top and firmer below, which showed that a heavier Apollo lander would not sink. Sources above.

### PARTS
1. **Footpads:** at the end of each of the three legs, one at each corner of the triangular frame. They took the landing shock and showed how firm the soil was. [read] https://science.nasa.gov/mission/surveyor-1/ and the NSSDCA URL
2. **TV survey camera:** near the top of the tripod frame. It took the 11,240 pictures, including views of its own footpad. [read] NSSDCA URL
   - Lens: JPL 2016 calls it a "slow-scan television imager with a zoom lens" [read, JPL 2016 URL]. NSSDCA experiment records describe a camera mounted under a mirror that moved in azimuth and elevation, with 25 mm and 100 mm lenses [snippet; the NSSDCA experiment pages returned an error when opened]. If you mention the mirror, flag it as unconfirmed.
3. **Mast with solar panel and flat (planar) antenna:** a central mast about 1 m above the top of the tripod. On it sit a solar array (792 cells, 0.855 m², up to 85 W) on a positioner at the very top, and a movable planar high-gain antenna near the top, which sent the TV pictures. [read] NSSDCA URL

## 2. Mars Pathfinder lander + Sojourner rover (landed 4 Jul 1997, Ares Vallis)

### DISCOVERY: Sojourner's APXS measured the chemistry of Mars rocks where they lay
- **Part:** the Alpha Proton X-ray Spectrometer (APXS), a sensor on a small deployment arm at the back of Sojourner. [read] https://science.nasa.gov/resource/sojourners-apxs-wedge/ (the rear colour camera image shows "the deployment arm for the APXS"). It measured all elements except hydrogen and helium above about 0.1% of the sample. [read] Pathfinder fact sheet https://mars.nasa.gov/internal_resources/815/
- **What it did:** on sol 3 Sojourner drove about 36 cm to the rock "Barnacle Bill" and held the APXS against it overnight. Over the next 2.5 months it measured 14 more rocks, including "Yogi" and "Scooby Doo", plus soils. [read] fact sheet URL. The mission returned "more than 15 chemical analyses of rocks and soil". [read] fact sheet
- **What humanity learned:** some rocks at the site "appear to be high in silica, suggesting differentiated parent materials". They differ from the Martian meteorites found on Earth. [read] fact sheet. Barnacle Bill's composition is described as andesitic (volcanic). [snippet] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia00634-sojourners-apxs-studies-barnacle-bill/
- **Last data:** 27 Sep 1997 (sol 83). [read] fact sheet; NSSDCA https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1996-068A

### QUESTION
- **Setup:** the cameras saw rounded pebbles and cobbles on the ground, and some rocks with pebbles stuck inside them and holes ("sockets") where pebbles used to be. How did the pebbles most likely get round?
- **A)** They were rolled and worn smooth in running water, long ago. **(correct)**
- **B)** Pathfinder's airbags rounded them while bouncing at landing.
- **C)** Sojourner's wheels ground them round.
- **Explanation:** the pebbles are also locked inside old rocks, so they were round long before any spacecraft arrived. NASA: the rounded pebbles and cobbles, and the sockets and pebbles in some rocks, "suggests conglomerates that formed in running water, during a warmer past in which liquid water was stable." [read] fact sheet. Keep the word "suggests": it is an interpretation, not a measurement. Do not offer "wind" as a wrong answer, because the same fact sheet credits wind-blown sand for grooved, fluted rocks at the site.
- **Alternative question (well sourced):** magnets on the lander collected airborne dust. Mars dust is magnetic; each particle is about 3 microns; the mineral is probably maghemite. [read] fact sheet

### PARTS
1. **IMP camera on its pop-up mast:** at the centre of the lander. It took panoramas in stereo and colour, and helped steer the rover. The NSSDCA gives a 0.8 m pop-up mast; the landing press kit gives the camera about 1.5 m above the ground. [read] NSSDCA URL; press kit https://mars.nasa.gov/internal_resources/816/. Fact sheet: more than 16,500 lander images and 550 rover images [read].
2. **Three petals with solar cells:** three triangular metal petals that open around the central body like a flower. Solar cells on them powered the lander, and the rover drove off one petal down a ramp. Solar area: three panels with 2.8 m² in total (press kit) vs "over 2.5 m" of solar cells (NSSDCA, per the summary I read). [read] NSSDCA URL; press kit URL
3. **Sojourner's APXS (back of the rover):** see the Discovery section. Sojourner is 65 cm long, 48 cm wide and 30 cm tall, with two black-and-white cameras at the front and a colour camera at the back. [read] fact sheet; press kit
- **Optional ASI/MET weather mast:** a 1 m mast with three windsocks [read, fact sheet]. The windsocks sit 33.1, 62.4 and 91.6 cm above the solar panel, so the mast stands on a petal [snippet] https://pds.nasa.gov/data/mpfl-m-asimet-4-ddr-edl-v1.0/mpam_0001/document/windinst.htm. I did not confirm which petal.

## 3. Spirit (MER-A, Gusev Crater)

### DISCOVERY: the dead wheel dug up almost pure silica
- **Part:** the right-front wheel. It stopped working in March 2006. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/right-front-wheel-rotations/. The Sol 2113 item gives sol 779 (13 Mar 2006). [snippet] https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/slight-movement-by-spirits-right-front-wheel-sol-2113/. Spirit then usually drove backwards, dragging the dead wheel. [snippet] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-mars-rover-spirit-faces-circuitous-route/
- **What it did:** the dragged wheel cut a trench and exposed bright soil from beneath the surface. The patch was nicknamed "Gertrude Weise", near "Home Plate"; the image is from sol 1,202, 21 May 2007. [read] https://science.nasa.gov/resource/rovers-wheel-churns-up-bright-martian-soil/ (PIA11758)
- **Measurement:** the APXS at the end of Spirit's arm measured about 90% pure silica. The Mini-TES had flagged it from a distance first. The silica is non-crystalline: no quartz. Announced 21 May 2007. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/mars-rover-spirit-unearths-surprise-evidence-of-wetter-past/. The NASA Science highlights page dates the find to "March 2007". [read] https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/science-highlights/. Use "announced May 2007".
- **What humanity learned:** silica this pure needs water to form, either from a hot spring or from soil reacting with acid volcanic vapours in the presence of water. [read] JPL release. On Earth both kinds of place (hot springs and fumaroles) teem with microbial life. [read] PIA11758. Scientist Albert Yen called it "some of the best evidence Spirit has found for water at Gusev". [read] JPL release
- **Backup discovery:** at "Comanche" (data from late 2005), rocks about 10 times richer in magnesium-iron carbonates than any Martian rock studied before. They formed when conditions were wet and non-acidic. [read] science-highlights URL

### QUESTION
- **Setup:** Spirit's broken front wheel no longer turned, so it scraped the ground like a plough. Behind it lay a strip of bright white soil from just under the surface. Spirit touched it with the chemistry sensor on its arm. What was it?
- **A)** Frozen water (ice).
- **B)** Almost pure silica, with the composition of opal. **(correct)**
- **C)** Fresh dust that had just blown in.
- **Explanation:** the APXS found about 90% silica, so it was not ice. It lay under the top layer, so it was not new dust. To make silica this pure you need water: hot springs or volcanic steam vents. [read] JPL release; PIA11758. Do not use "salt" as a distractor: the release says the wheel exposed several bright patches and I did not check what the others were made of.

### PARTS
1. **Right-front wheel:** front right corner of the rover. It stopped in 2006 and was dragged, which exposed the silica. [read] URLs above
2. **Pancam on the mast:** colour panoramic cameras on top of the mast, about 1.5 m (5 ft) high, with the two cameras about 30 cm apart. [read] https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/science-instruments/. Another NASA page gives about 1.4 m measured from the base of the wheels. [snippet] https://science.nasa.gov/resource/mars-exploration-rover-panoramic-camera-mast-assembly/
3. **Tool turret at the end of the robotic arm:** carries the APXS (chemistry), the Mössbauer spectrometer (iron minerals), the Microscopic Imager and the RAT (grinding wheel). [read] science-instruments URL. The arm is attached to the lower front of the rover's body (the warm electronics box) and reaches out in front of the rover. The Pancam mast stands at the front of the top deck. [read, Opportunity record; Spirit is a twin] https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=2003-032A

## 4. Opportunity (MER-B, Meridiani Planum)

### DISCOVERY: the "Homestake" gypsum vein, a "slam-dunk" for flowing water
- **Part:** the APXS and Microscopic Imager on the arm, and the Pancam on the mast. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-mars-rover-finds-mineral-vein-deposited-by-water/
- **What it did:** at "Cape York", on the western rim of Endeavour Crater (about 22 km wide [read, highlights URL]), Opportunity examined a bright vein about as wide as a thumb (1–2 cm) and 40–50 cm long (45 cm in the photo caption). The APXS found abundant calcium and sulfur in a ratio pointing to fairly pure calcium sulfate, and Pancam filters suggested gypsum. Announced 7 Dec 2011. [read] JPL release
- **What humanity learned:** water flowed through underground cracks and left the mineral there, possibly under more neutral (less acidic) conditions than at Opportunity's earlier sites. No similar veins appeared on the 33 km of plains it crossed over 90 months. Steve Squyres called it a "slam-dunk story" of water. [read] JPL release
- **Earlier result (Eagle Crater, announced 2 Mar 2004):** the Mössbauer spectrometer found jarosite, a mineral that holds water, and the APXS found very high sulfur. Scientists concluded the area was once "soaking wet". [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/opportunity-rover-finds-strong-evidence-meridiani-planum-was-wet/

### QUESTION (the "blueberries")
- **Setup:** in the rock layers of Eagle Crater, Opportunity's cameras saw many tiny round balls, about the size of BBs, nicknamed "blueberries". They were spread through the rock, not packed into one layer. How did they form?
- **A)** Drops of molten rock thrown out by a volcano.
- **B)** Drops of molten rock splashed out by a meteorite impact.
- **C)** Minerals that slowly grew inside rock soaked with water. **(correct)**
- **Explanation:** these were the three real ideas scientists weighed in March 2004. Because the balls were not concentrated in particular layers, the volcano and impact ideas looked less likely, though not yet fully ruled out. [read] 2 Mar 2004 JPL release. Later, the rover's spectrometers confirmed the balls are rich in hematite, "deposited in water" (rock "Berry Bowl", March 2004). [read] https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/discovery-served-up-in-a-bowl/ (PIA05634). Close-up from the Microscopic Imager on the arm, sol 84, 19 Apr 2004, near Fram Crater. [read] https://science.nasa.gov/resource/martian-blueberries-2/
- **Size warning:** the NASA Science highlights page appears to give 3 cm for the spherules. But the blueberries resource page gives 3 cm as the width of the whole image [read]. Use "BB-sized", from the 2004 release, and not 3 cm.

### PARTS
1. **Tool turret at the end of the robotic arm (RAT, APXS, Microscopic Imager, Mössbauer):** the RAT grinds off dust and weathered rock; the others read chemistry, minerals and close-ups. The RAT also carries 4 of the 7 dust magnets. [read] science-instruments URL. The arm is attached to the lower front of the body and reaches out in front. [read] https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=2003-032A
2. **Pancam on the mast:** on top of the mast, about 1.5 m high; the mast stands at the front of the top deck. It took the colour images of the Homestake vein. [read] science-instruments URL; NSSDCA 2003-032A; JPL Homestake release
- **Context:** two solar panels fixed to the front edges of the deck, level with it, look like "swept-back wings" when extended (up to 140 W). [read] NSSDCA 2003-032A
3. **Dust magnets:** two on the front of the rover (a capture magnet and a filter magnet) and one "sweep" magnet on top of the deck, in view of Pancam. [read] science-instruments URL

## 5. Phoenix (landed 25 May 2008, Mars arctic, about 68°N)

### DISCOVERY: the wet chemistry lab found perchlorate and alkaline soil
- **Part:** the robotic arm scooped soil and delivered it to the Wet Chemistry Laboratory, part of MECA on the lander deck. The arm's scoop dropped up to 1 cm³ of soil into a drawer that tipped it into a beaker of melted water brought from Earth, and a paddle stirred it while sensors measured the dissolved ions. [read] press kit (design description, pre-landing) https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/phoenix-landing.pdf. First results, with a preliminary pH of 8–9: [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/phoenix-returns-treasure-trove-for-science/ (26 Jun 2008)
- **Numbers (peer-reviewed; I read the abstracts on the University of Arizona repository pages):**
  - Perchlorate was 0.4–0.6% by mass, with about 10 mM of dissolved salts; the soil was moderately alkaline, pH 7.7 ± 0.5. Hecht et al., Science 325:64–67 (2009), doi:10.1126/science.1172466. https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/detection-of-perchlorate-and-the-soluble-chemistry-of-martian-soi/
  - pH 7.7 ± 0.3; perchlorate was the dominant anion in solution. Kounaves et al., JGR 115, E00E10 (2010), doi:10.1029/2009JE003424. https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/wet-chemistry-experiments-on-the-2007-phoenix-mars-scout-lander-m/
  - NASA's 2009 summary: "a few tenths of a percent" in all three soil samples analysed. Perchlorate strongly attracts water and could pull humidity from the air. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-phoenix-results-point-to-martian-climate-cycles/
- **What humanity learned:** Martian soil holds a reactive salt that soaks up water, and the soil is not acidic. In Michael Hecht's words, the work moved Mars research from "follow the water" to "follow the chemistry". [read] 2009 JPL URL
- **Other verified results you could use instead:**
  - TEGA oven, announced 31 Jul 2008: "We have water" (William Boynton). The robotic arm delivered the sample from a trench about 2 in deep. [read] https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/text/phoenix_pr_20080731.txt
  - Calcium carbonate, a sign of past liquid water. [read] 2009 JPL URL
  - Lidar snow, release of 29 Sep 2008: snow fell from clouds about 4 km up and vaporised before reaching the ground. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-mars-lander-sees-falling-snow-soil-data-suggest-liquid-past/. Keep this out of the quiz: later reports say precipitation reached the ground on sol 109 (unverified, see below).

### QUESTION (the vanishing chunks)
- **Setup:** the robotic arm widened the trench "Dodo-Goldilocks" on sol 20 (15 Jun 2008), uncovering dice-sized bright white chunks. Four sols later (sol 24) the lander's stereo camera looked again, and several chunks were gone. What were they?
- **A)** Salt crystals.
- **B)** Water ice that turned straight into vapour. **(correct)**
- **C)** Small white pebbles.
- **Explanation:** salt or pebbles would not vanish in a few days; ice would. Peter Smith: "It must be ice" and "Salt can't do that." The ice sublimated (solid straight to gas) once digging exposed it to the thin, dry air. [read] https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/bright-chunks-at-phoenix-landers-mars-site-must-have-been-ice/; caption (SSI camera, sol 24 = 19 Jun 2008) https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/ice-on-mars-now-its-gone (PIA10911). Do not offer dry ice as an option: it would vanish too, and no source I read excludes it.

### PARTS
1. **Robotic arm with scoop:** 2.35 m (7.7 ft) long. One end is attached to the lander deck, with an elbow in the middle; the other end has a scoop with blades and a powered rasp for frozen soil. It dug the trenches and fed samples to the deck instruments. [read] press kit. Other NASA pages say "nearly 8-foot (2.5-meter)". [snippet]
2. **Surface Stereo Imager on its mast:** twin cameras on a mast about 2 m (7 ft) above the ground. They took the before-and-after ice pictures. [read] press kit; PIA10911
3. **Weather (MET) mast with telltale:** a small wind telltale hangs from the top of the mast and the stereo camera photographs it to measure wind. The top of the mast, 1.14 m above the deck, is the lander's highest point. The same kit also calls it a "1.2-meter (4-foot) mast", and Aarhus University (Denmark) built the telltale. [read] press kit
- **Context:** two round (octagonal) solar wings extend from opposite sides of the deck. [read] NSSDCA https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=2007-034A; press kit

## Unverified or dropped
- **Surveyor camera mirror:** only in a snippet from NSSDCA records; the page would not open. The "fear of deep dust" is sourced, but I found no source naming Thomas Gold, so I left him out.
- **Surveyor 1 scoop:** search results mention a scoop proving the soil firm. That was Surveyor 3 and later, not Surveyor 1 (JPL 2016). Do not attribute it to Surveyor 1.
- **Spirit "drove backwards dragging the wheel":** the idea that Spirit drove backwards came only from a search snippet. The silica release I read says only that the wheel drags. A failure date of "April 2006" appeared in search results without an identifiable URL, so it is dropped.
- **Pathfinder:** "Barnacle Bill is andesitic" is a snippet only. The ASI/MET mast position on the lander is unconfirmed.
- **MER arm and mast positions:** now confirmed on the NSSDCA Opportunity record (2003-032A). I did not open the Spirit record, but the rovers are twins.
- **Phoenix:** the trench name for the 31 Jul 2008 TEGA sample was not given. The lidar's position on the lander was not stated. "Snow reached the ground on sol 109" appears only in press coverage of Whiteway et al. 2009, so it is dropped.
- **Not used:** a patent, arXiv papers, Universe Today, and NTRS numbers I did not read (0.35 kg/cm², N/cm² profiles).

## 1. Pioneer 10

### DISCOVERY: Jupiter's radiation belts, measured by a craft that barely got through
- Part: the charged-particle instruments (a "trapped radiation detector" was one of the 11 instruments) [HIST-P10]. Pioneer 10 measured "Jupiter's magnetosphere, radiation belts, magnetic field, atmosphere, and interior" [MDSCC-P10].
- Before the flyby, scientists worried Jupiter's radiation "might be so hostile that a spacecraft could not survive a close flyby" (paper titled "Can Pioneer 10 survive?", Nov 1973) [HESS-1973].
- What happened: Jupiter's radiation caused "false commands"; the mission met all objectives except one; the imaging photopolarimeter "succumbed to radiation", so no close images of Io were obtained [SCI-P10]. An Io image was "lost to the intense radiation environment" [HIST-P10].
- What humanity learned: these radiation measurements were "crucial in designing the Voyager and Galileo spacecraft" [MDSCC-P10].
- Dates and numbers: closest approach 02:26 UT on 4 Dec 1973, 130,354 km (81,000 mi) from the cloud tops, about 126,000 km/h [SCI-P10]. CONFLICT on date and distance: 3 Dec 1973 (US date) [MDSCC-P10]; 82,178 mi (about 132,000 km) [HIST-P10]; 04 Dec 1973 at "2.8 Jovian radii (about 200,000 km)", which is measured from Jupiter's centre, not the cloud tops [NSSDCA-P10]. Safe wording: "early December 1973, about 130,000 km above the clouds."
- Bonus fact (same flyby, other instruments): data from the Feb 1976 crossing of Saturn's orbit indicated Jupiter's magnetic tail is "almost 800 million kilometers long" [SCI-P10].
- DROP: "radiation 10,000 times Earth's" (found only in non-NASA articles and an unreadable SP-349 PDF; not verified). DROP: "nearly damaged electronics" as a direct quote; use "false commands" and "lost the Io pictures" instead.

### QUESTION: the Pioneer anomaly (applies to Pioneer 10 AND 11; use it on one page only)
- Setup: Navigators tracking the Pioneers saw them slowing down a tiny bit more than gravity alone could explain, a pull back toward the Sun of about 0.9 nanometres per second squared. Navigators first noticed it in the early 1980s; John Anderson's JPL group identified it in 1998 [JPL-ANOM].
- A) Leftover fuel dribbling out of the pipes was pushing the craft (this was what early navigators blamed) [JPL-ANOM]
- B) A new kind of physics that breaks Einstein's theory of gravity (raised as a possibility in 1998) [JPL-ANOM]
- C) Heat from the spacecraft's own electronics and plutonium power units pushing it very gently [JPL-ANOM]
- Correct: C.
- Explanation: a JPL-led study (Slava Turyshev et al., Physical Review Letters, published 12 June 2012; JPL release 17 July 2012) calculated the heat from "electrical current flowing through instruments and the thermoelectric power supply" and from the decay of plutonium in the power sources; the push from that heat matched the observed slowdown on both spacecraft [JPL-ANOM]. Note: the JPL release does NOT mention heat reflecting off the back of the antenna; do not add that detail.

### PARTS (Pioneer 10 and 11 share the same design)
1. High-gain dish antenna: talked to Earth. Location: the big dish on the front; the boxy equipment compartment sits behind it. 2.74 m wide, 46 cm deep [NSSDCA-P10]; "2.7-m (9-ft.)" dish attached to the hexagonal front face of the equipment compartment; its feed sits on three struts about 1.2 m in front of the dish rim [KIT-79].
2. Nuclear power units (RTGs): made electricity from plutonium-238 heat, because sunlight at Jupiter is too weak for practical solar panels [HIST-P10; KIT-79 says "sunlight at Jupiter and beyond is too weak for an efficient solar-powered system"]. Location: on two arms (three-rod trusses, 120 degrees apart) about 3 m from the centre [KIT-79; NSSDCA-P10]. CONFLICT on count: four SNAP-19 units [NSSDCA-P10] vs two SNAP-19 [SCI-P10] and two RTGs [HIST-P10]; the 1979 press kit gives no count [KIT-79]. Safe wording: "power units on two long arms" (do not state a number).
3. Magnetometer boom: held the helium vector magnetometer far away from the craft's own magnetism. Location: the third, longest, thin arm, 120 degrees from each RTG arm, sensor 6.6 m from the spacecraft centre [KIT-79; NSSDCA-P10 says the boom extended 6.6 m].
- Plaque (already on site): "attached to this trusswork", i.e. the external tube-frame that supports the dish [KIT-79]. Size 152 x 229 mm (from a NASA Historical Data Book search snippet only; NOT read directly).

## 2. Pioneer 11

### DISCOVERY: a new ring at Saturn, found with a 2.5 cm telescope
- Part: the imaging photopolarimeter, "including a 2.5-centimeter telescope", run continuously for two weeks in Aug-Sep 1979 [GEHRELS-1980].
- What it did: found a new ring at 2.33 Saturn radii, "provisionally named the F ring", separated from the A ring by the provisionally named "Pioneer division"; also found a satellite, 1979 S 1, at 2.53 Saturn radii [GEHRELS-1980]. NASA: discovered "a narrow ring outside the A ring named the F ring" and a new moon about 200 km in diameter [SCI-P11]; another NASA page says "two new moons", "almost smacking into one of them" in Sep 1979 [SCI-P11-SAT]. CONFLICT: one new moon [SCI-P11] vs two [SCI-P11-SAT; HIST-P11-45 "two new moons and one new ring"]. The moon's modern name and the near-miss distance were NOT verified: DROP.
- Flyby numbers: closest approach 16:31 UT, 1 Sep 1979, about 20,900 km (13,000 mi) from the cloud tops, about 114,000 km/h; ring plane crossed at 14:36 UT [SCI-P11]. NSSDCA gives 21,000 km [NSSDCA-P11]; the 1979 press kit planned 21,400 km [KIT-79].
- Also: detected Saturn's bow shock on 31 Aug 1979, about 1.5 million km out, the "first conclusive evidence" of Saturn's magnetic field [SCI-P11]. Titan average temperature about -193 C (-315 F) [SCI-P11].
- Ring-plane path tested for Voyager (lead CONFIRMED): planners chose the safer pass outside the rings over a riskier inner-ring pass because the outer route was a pathfinder for Voyager 2, which "had to take that same trajectory" for its gravity assist to Uranus and Neptune [HIST-P11-45]. The 1979 press kit: "Since the existence of the E ring is critical to Voyager, NASA officials determined that the maximum return from all missions would be achieved by targeting Pioneer Saturn outside the rings"; one goal was to find "safe trajectories for Voyager" using "the ring-plane passage of Pioneer itself"; ring-plane passage would take about 0.8 s and "an impact with a fragment of the E ring could destroy the spacecraft" [KIT-79].

### QUESTION: how is Saturn's magnet lined up?
- Setup: Pioneer 11 carried a magnetometer on a long boom and measured Saturn's magnetic field for the first time. Earth's magnetic axis is tilted 11.2 degrees from its spin axis [GSFC-MAG]. How is Saturn's magnetic axis tilted compared with its spin axis?
- A) Tilted about the same as Earth's (around 11 degrees)
- B) Almost perfectly lined up with the spin axis (less than 1 degree)
- C) Tipped over sideways, almost at a right angle
- Correct: B.
- Explanation: from Pioneer 11's vector helium magnetometer data, "the tilt angle between the magnetic dipole and the rotation axis is less than 1 deg" [SMITH-1980]; the trapped-particle instrument independently indicated "a centered dipole aligned with the planetary rotation axis" [SIMPSON-1980]. NASA Goddard: "Saturn's magnetic axis seems exactly aligned with its rotation axis, within the errors of the observations" [GSFC-MAG]. Jupiter's is tilted "about the same amount as the Earth's" [GSFC-MAG], so Saturn is the odd one out.

### PARTS
Same body as Pioneer 10 (dish, two RTG arms, magnetometer boom; see Pioneer 10 PARTS) [KIT-79]. Pioneer-11-specific notes:
1. High-gain dish antenna: big dish on the front. 2.74 m [NSSDCA-P11]; press kit says both "2.7-m" and "2.75-meter (9-foot)" [KIT-79].
2. Magnetometer on the long thin boom: measured Saturn's magnetic field. "Helium vector magnetometer" whose sensor sits on a mast 6.5 m (21.5 ft) from the centre (another page of the same kit: 6.6 m) [KIT-79]. Pioneer 11 also had a fluxgate magnetometer (Goddard) [KIT-79; SCI-P11].
3. RTGs on the two other arms: about 144 W at Jupiter, 100 W at Saturn [NSSDCA-P11]. One RTG boom had a "momentary failure" to deploy after launch [SCI-P11].
- Imaging photopolarimeter (found the F ring): exact mounting spot not verified. KIT-79 says most experiments sit in the smaller instrument compartment joined to the main hexagonal box behind the dish, with openings for their sensors. Safe marker: "on the instrument box behind the dish."

## 3. NEAR Shoemaker

### DISCOVERY: reading Eros's chemistry from the ground
- Part: the gamma-ray spectrometer (part of the X-ray/gamma-ray spectrometer). After touchdown on 12 Feb 2001 it "collected data from the asteroid's surface" [NSSDCA-NEAR] from "an ideal vantage point about four inches from the surface"; "the first gamma-ray experiment that has ever been done on the surface of a body other than Earth"; data for "seven days after the spacecraft landed" [NEAR-PR-2001]. It could sense composition to a depth of about 10 cm, and the surface data showed iron, oxygen, silicon and potassium; the sunlit solar panels kept it powered [APOD-2001].
- Correction to the lead: "four inches" appears as BOTH the stand-off distance [NEAR-PR-2001] and the sensing depth (about 10 cm) [APOD-2001]. They are different things; both are sourced.
- What humanity learned: a reanalysis of the NEAR X-ray/gamma-ray data found Eros's composition "consistent with the L and LL ordinary chondrites", the commonest kind of stony meteorite; the first in-situ hydrogen measurement on an asteroid; the landing site's composition "indistinguishable from the mean surface" [PEPLOWSKI-2015, abstract only]. Plain words: Eros is made of the same kind of rock as a well-known family of stony meteorites found on Earth (ordinary chondrites). (Calling them "the most common" meteorites is general knowledge, not from a source I read.)
- Landing numbers: 12 Feb 2001, about 6.4 km/h (4 mph), 69 high-resolution photos during descent, south of the saddle feature Himeros [SCI-NEAR]. CONFLICT on time: 19:44 UT [SCI-NEAR] vs 20:01:52 UT [NSSDCA-NEAR] (the latter is probably Earth-received time; not confirmed).
- DROP: NEAR Laser Rangefinder "mapped the shape": the instrument is listed [SCI-NEAR] but no result was read. Eros size is 13 x 13 x 33 km [NSSDCA-NEAR] (the site's "34 km" is close but NSSDCA says 33).

### QUESTION: does Eros have a magnetic field?
- Setup: NEAR carried a magnetometer and kept checking for a magnetic field from orbit and, after landing, right on the surface.
- A) A strong magnetic field like Earth's
- B) A weak field, like a fridge magnet
- C) No magnetic field it could detect
- Correct: C.
- Explanation: NEAR's magnetometer data showed Eros had no magnetic field [SCI-NEAR]; a brief magnetometer check on the surface found no discernible field [APOD-2001].
- Alternative question (composition): "Eros is most like: A) iron meteorites B) ordinary stony meteorites (ordinary chondrites) C) a comet's ice and dust" -> B [PEPLOWSKI-2015]. Weaker: rests on a paper abstract, not a NASA page.

### PARTS
1. Four solar panels: made electricity from sunlight; NEAR was the first spacecraft to rely on solar cells beyond Mars's orbit [SCI-NEAR]. Location: four fixed panels sticking out in a "windmill arrangement" around the body, each 1.8 x 1.2 m [NSSDCA-NEAR]. NEAR probably landed resting on the tips of its panels [APOD-2001].
2. High-gain dish antenna with the magnetometer: sent data to Earth; the magnetometer is "mounted on the antenna feed" [NSSDCA-NEAR]. Location: the fixed 1.5 m dish on one end, with the magnetometer at the dish's centre feed [NSSDCA-NEAR].
3. Science instruments (gamma-ray spectrometer, camera, laser rangefinder, infrared spectrometer): Location: "fixed on the opposite end (the aft deck)" from the X-ray solar monitor on the forward deck [NSSDCA-NEAR]. Body is "an octagonal prism, approximately 1.7 m on a side" [NSSDCA-NEAR].

## 4. Dawn

### DISCOVERY: the bright spots of Ceres are salt from deep brine
- Part: the visible and infrared mapping spectrometer (VIR) [PIA20694; JPL-2016].
- What it did: VIR found "the dominant mineral of this bright area is sodium carbonate"; Occator is "the highest concentration of carbonate minerals ever seen outside Earth" [JPL-2016; PIA20694]. Occator is 92 km (57 mi) wide with a central pit about 10 km wide [JPL-2016]. A 2015 hypothesis of magnesium sulfate was superseded [JPL-2016].
- What humanity learned: using gravity data and imaging from Dawn's final orbit "less than 22 miles (35 kilometers) above the surface", scientists concluded the liquid came from a deep brine (salty water) reservoir about 40 km (25 mi) deep and hundreds of miles wide; Cerealia Facula also has salts including sodium chloride bound with water and ammonium chloride [JPL-2020]. Ceres may have had interior hydrothermal activity [JPL-2016].
- CONFLICT on ages: some bright regions "less than 2 million years old", Occator about 20 million years old [JPL-2020] vs Cerealia Facula about 4 million years and the crater about 34 million years [JPL-2017-AGE, search snippet only]. Safe wording: "a few million years old, very young for a solar-system object."
- Alternative discovery (Vesta): Rheasilvia basin 500 km (310 mi) wide, about 95% of Vesta's mean diameter; central peak rises 12-16 miles (about 19-26 km); Dawn confirmed Vesta is the parent body of the HED meteorites, about 6% of all meteorites found on Earth; iron core about 140 miles across [SCI-VESTA]. Vesta "is the source of more meteorites on Earth than Mars or the moon" [JPL-DAWN].

### QUESTION: what are Ceres's bright spots?
- Setup: Dawn's pictures of Ceres showed very bright spots inside Occator crater.
- A) Fresh ice from a comet that crashed there
- B) Salt left behind by salty water that came up from inside Ceres
- C) Shiny metal from a crashed metal asteroid
- Correct: B.
- Explanation: Dawn's VIR spectrometer showed the bright material is mostly sodium carbonate, a salt [JPL-2016; PIA20694]; gravity data showed it came from a brine reservoir about 40 km down [JPL-2020].
- Caution: option A is not completely absurd (Ceres's bright salts include sodium chloride bound with water [JPL-2020]); keep the correct answer worded as "mostly salt."

### PARTS
Location note: the positions below come from the NSSDCA Dawn page, which also lists hardware Dawn never flew (see WARNING), so it reads like a pre-launch description. Sizes are confirmed by science.nasa.gov; confirm positions against the 3D model.
1. Two solar wings: powered the spacecraft and its ion engines. Each 8.3 x 2.3 m; 19.7-20 m tip to tip; more than 10 kW near Earth [SCI-DAWN-SC]. Location: two long wings "mounted on opposite sides of the spacecraft" [NSSDCA-DAWN].
2. Ion engines: three xenon ion thrusters, 33 cm long, 41 cm wide, 19-91 mN of thrust; 425 kg of xenon at launch [SCI-DAWN-SC]; only one runs at a time (2007 press kit, search snippet). Location: "two-axis gimbal mounted at the base of the spacecraft" [NSSDCA-DAWN]: the bottom of the box.
3. Instrument deck with framing camera and VIR: Location: on the top panel ("instrument bench" holding the cameras and mapping spectrometer); the gamma-ray/neutron detector (GRaND) is on the top panel too [NSSDCA-DAWN]. High-gain antenna: 1.52 m dish "mounted on one side of the spacecraft in the same plane as the solar arrays" [NSSDCA-DAWN; SCI-DAWN-SC for 1.52 m].
- WARNING: NSSDCA-DAWN also lists a "laser altimeter" and a 5 m "magnetometer boom". These are not among Dawn's instruments on NASA's mission pages (FC, VIR, GRaND only [SCI-VESTA navigation; JPL-DAWN instrument list]); they look like early design items. Do not put them on the model.
- End: hydrazine ran out on 31 Oct 2018; mission end 1 Nov 2018 [SCI-DAWN-SC].

## 5. Kepler

### DISCOVERY: the commonest kind of planet is one our solar system lacks
- Part: the photometer, a Schmidt telescope with a 0.95 m aperture, 1.4 m primary mirror and 42 CCDs (about 95 megapixels), "the largest camera NASA has ever flown in space" at the time; 105-square-degree field of view [KEP-KIT]. CONFLICT (rounding): "3-foot (1-meter) diameter aperture" [SCI-KEP-ID]. NSSDCA gives a "12-degree field of view" on the "Cygnus-Lyra region" [NSSDCA-KEP]; the press kit says 105 sq deg = 12-degree diameter [KEP-KIT]. One search result (not read directly, origin unclear) says 115 sq deg; use 105.
- What it did: stared at about 100,000 stars in Cygnus-Lyra, looking for transits [KEP-KIT].
- What humanity learned: "The most common size of planet Kepler found" is between the size of Earth and Neptune, a kind of planet that does not exist in our solar system; 20-50% of the stars visible in the night sky are likely to have small, possibly rocky planets in their habitable zones [NASA-KEP-RET]; there are more planets than stars in our galaxy [SCI-KEP]. More than 2,600 planet discoveries [NASA-KEP-RET; SCI-KEP].
- Dates: launched 6 Mar 2009 [SCI-KEP]; one reaction wheel failed in July 2012, and a second was found failed after safe-mode events in May 2013; at least three were needed for precise pointing [SCI-KEP-ID]; K2 approved May 2014, data from 30 May 2014 [SCI-KEP-ID]; K2 used sunlight pressure to hold its pointing, "like a kayak steering into the current", with 19 campaigns along the ecliptic [SCI-KEP]; retirement announced 30 Oct 2018 [NASA-KEP-RET].

### QUESTION: how much does a star dim?
- Setup: Kepler watched for stars that dim when a planet passes in front of them. When an Earth-size planet crosses a Sun-size star, how much dimmer does the star get?
- A) About half as bright
- B) About 10% dimmer
- C) Less than one hundredth of one percent dimmer
- Correct: C.
- Explanation: an Earth-size planet crossing a Sun-size star dims it "only 84 parts per million" (0.0084%), for "a few hours to half a day"; Kepler's photometer could detect changes as small as 20 ppm; NASA compares it to "a mosquito crossing in front of a car's headlight"; Kepler needed at least three transits to count a signal as an Earth-size planet candidate [KEP-KIT].

### PARTS
1. Telescope tube with sunshade: collected starlight. Location: the tall tube that "sits atop the spacecraft"; the slanted sunshade is "mounted at the front of the photometer" (the open top end) [KEP-KIT].
2. Solar panels: made up to 1,100 W and shaded the telescope from direct sunlight; fixed, not unfolded; four non-coplanar panels, 10.2 square metres [KEP-KIT]. Location: "rigidly mounted to the spacecraft's upper deck" and it "shades the photometer" [KEP-KIT]; in NASA pictures it wraps along the sun-facing side of the telescope tube (my reading of images, not stated in text).
3. High-gain dish antenna: sent data to Earth; about every 30 days the whole spacecraft turned to point it at Earth [KEP-KIT]. Location: on the hexagonal box (spacecraft bus) at the base of the telescope; the bus's six side panels hold "high- and low-gain antennas" [KEP-KIT].
- Reaction wheels (inside the bus, not visible): four wheels; their failures led to K2 [KEP-KIT; SCI-KEP-ID].

- **Coordinates.** S-V1 gives 22.483 N, 47.94 W without naming a frame. That is the same point as NSSDCA's 312.05 E. The 22.48 latitude is most likely planetographic (planetographic is about 0.2 degrees larger at this latitude). The JS uses the NSSDCA planetocentric value.
- **"First successful US Mars lander".** S-V1 says Viking 1 made "the first truly successful landing on Mars" and notes that the Soviet Mars 3 touched down in 1971 but went silent seconds later. The JS avoids the "first" claim.
- **Cause of the faulty command.** Other sites say it was a battery-software update that overwrote antenna-pointing data. I did not find that on a NASA page, so the JS says only "a faulty command sent from Earth".

## 4. Ingenuity helicopter

| Field | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival (`land`) | Perseverance touchdown, carrying Ingenuity under its belly: 18 Feb 2021, 20:55 UT, which N-M20 states is Earth-received time | N-M20 |
| Site | Jezero crater, "about 18 degrees north latitude, 77 degrees longitude", 45 km wide | PK-M20 |

| Final spot | "Valinor Hills", Jezero crater | JPL-ING-BYE |
| Hardware | 1.8 kg; two counter-rotating rotors, four 1.2 m blades, about 2,400 rpm; solar array on top of the rotors charging six Li-ion batteries (enough for one 90 s flight per sol); black-and-white navigation camera + colour camera; four legs; Mars air about 1% of Earth's surface density | PK-ING, 6TK, S-ING |
| Lesson | The navigation system worked over textured (pebbly) flat ground and failed over featureless ripples; the findings are "expected to benefit future Mars helicopters" | JPL-ING-ACC |

Disagreements and open points:
- **Last signal (unresolved).** JPL-ING-BYE describes a final communication session with the team on 16 Apr 2024. JPL-ING-ACC (Dec 2024) says Ingenuity "still beams weather and avionics test data to the Perseverance rover about once a week". So there is no clear last-signal date. The JS uses **"Último vuelo / Last flight: 18 Jan 2024"**, as data.js does, with `lastYear: 2024`.
- **Coordinates (only partly verified).** NASA gives only "about 18 N, 77 E". The JS uses 18.44, 77.45, the Perseverance landing site (Octavia E. Butler Landing, 18.4447 N, 77.4508 E). That value appears only on Wikipedia, not on a NASA page. It is where Ingenuity arrived, **not** where it rests: Valinor Hills is elsewhere in Jezero, and I did not find its coordinates on a NASA page. On a globe the gap is small, but the site should not claim the marker is the resting place.
- **Rotor speed.** The press kit, 6TK and the fact sheet say about 2,400 rpm. A JPL image page says flights used 2,537 rpm, later raised to 2,700. N-M20 says "almost 3000 rpm". The JS says "unas 2.400 vueltas por minuto o más / about 2,400 turns a minute or more".
- **Not found on NASA pages:** the date Ingenuity was set down on the ground (3 Apr 2021 appears only on Wikipedia mirrors) and the Tolkien origin of "Valinor Hills". Neither is used.
- `land` is Perseverance's landing because Ingenuity had no separate landing. If the site prefers the first flight, use `2021-04-19T07:34:00Z` (N-M20).

## Writing notes
- Quiz correct indices: apollo11 1, viking1 0, insight 2, ingenuity 1. The order of `o` and `a` is the same in es and en.
- The wrong options are false, not half-true. For InSight, the question is about the mole, not the shutdown, so that the "dust" and "batteries" answers, which are both true, do not compete.
- Spanish numbers use the house format: 1.319; 4.500; 2.400; 3,8; 2,5; 1,4; 2,2; 1,2; 39,1; 1,8.
- Strings contain no em dashes or exclamation marks. Voseo is not needed because no string gives an instruction.
